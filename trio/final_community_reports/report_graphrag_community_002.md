# Community Report: Levant and Mesopotamia Conflict Zone

## Executive Summary

This report analyzes a chronologically stratified sample of conflict events from the Levant and Mesopotamia region, spanning from January 2016 to December 2023. The data reveals a highly volatile and complex security environment characterized by multi-faceted conflicts involving state, non-state, and international actors. Key conflict drivers include counter-terrorism operations, the Syrian civil war, and regional power dynamics, leading to significant fatalities, widespread displacement, and persistent instability. The severity metrics indicate an enduring state of extreme conflict, with a very high risk of further escalation across the region.

## Primary Actors & Dynamics

The conflict landscape is dominated by a diverse array of actors, each with distinct roles and objectives:

*   **Global Coalition Against Daesh (GCAD):** Primarily active in Iraq and Syria, conducting extensive air/drone strikes targeting Islamic State (IS) positions, frequently resulting in high fatalities among IS militants and, at times, significant civilian casualties. GCAD also engages in movement of forces to support local allies.
*   **Military Forces of Syria (and loyalist militias):** Engaged in widespread armed clashes, airstrikes, and territorial operations against various opposition and jihadist groups, including IS and Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS). Noteworthy for reported chemical weapon use and extensive arrests, often targeting displaced Syrians.
*   **Islamic State in Iraq and the Levant (ISIL):** A persistent and highly lethal non-state actor, responsible for numerous suicide bombings, direct attacks, and armed clashes against military forces and civilians across Syria and Iraq. Their activities are characterized by high fatality counts and a focus on territorial control and destabilization.
*   **Syrian Democratic Forces (QSD):** A key ground force in northeastern Syria, predominantly clashing with IS for territorial control, often supported by GCAD airstrikes. They also engage in various security measures and movement of forces.
*   **Military Forces of Russia:** A critical external intervener, primarily conducting airstrikes in Syria in support of the Syrian regime, targeting opposition and jihadist factions. Also involved in movement of forces and patrols.
*   **Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS):** A prominent jihadist group in Syria, involved in armed clashes and territorial control efforts against regime forces and other factions, as well as IED-related incidents.

Other notable actors include the military forces of Lebanon and Israel, various Syrian opposition rebels, pro-Iranian militias, and other unidentified armed groups, contributing to the fragmented and multi-layered nature of the conflict.

## Temporal Evolution (2016-2023)

**2016:** The year begins with significant counter-terrorism activities in Lebanon by the military forces, focusing on arrests and disrupted weapons use against suspected militants and Syrian nationals. In Iraq, the Global Coalition intensified air/drone strikes against IS, particularly around Ramadi, Shirqat, Kirkuk, and Mosul, leading to numerous IS fatalities. Sporadic incidents involving Israeli forces and pro-government militias occurred in Lebanon and Bahrain, respectively. Jordan also reported a weapons seizure.

**2017:** A stark shift to large-scale, high-fatality events, predominantly in Syria. IS launched devastating suicide attacks and territorial offensives (Azaz, Deir-ez-Zor, Sosyan). Global Coalition airstrikes in Iraq (Mosul, Ramadi) and Syria (Idleb, Raqqa, Deir-ez-Zor, Al-Bukamal) continued to inflict heavy casualties on IS but also resulted in significant civilian deaths (e.g., Mosul - Al-Jadeda with 230 fatalities). The Syrian military was implicated in a chemical weapon attack in Khan Shaykun. HTS and Liwa al-Aqsa were engaged in major clashes and mass killings of captured fighters. Hezbollah was active in clashes and territorial gains in Lebanon/Syria border areas.

**2018:** The conflict remained highly intense in Syria. Massive armed clashes occurred between the Syrian military (including the 4th Armored Division, often backed by Russia) and IS, as well as various rebel factions, resulting in hundreds of fatalities in single events (e.g., Hama, Idleb, Khasham, Al-Bukamal, Damascus). Russian and Coalition airstrikes continued across various locations. Disturbingly, multiple events reported high fatalities (60-80+) in Sidnaya Military Prison, attributed to Syrian military forces (prison guards), indicating severe human rights abuses. Turkish forces engaged QSD/YPG in Afrin.

**2019:** While large-scale combat continued, there was a visible mix of high-fatality engagements and non-lethal security and movement events. IS and Syrian forces clashed, notably in Al-Sayyal (60 fatalities). QSD, supported by the Global Coalition, continued operations against IS in Baghuz, with Coalition airstrikes causing hundreds of fatalities among IS families and members. The Syrian military executed 95 detainees in Sidnaya Prison. However, the latter part of the year saw an increase in non-violent activities such as troop movements, security measures, arrests, and civilian displacement in response to military operations. Unexploded ordnance (UXO) incidents began to appear more frequently.

**2020:** The sample for this year shows a decrease in very high-fatality events, though significant clashes occurred (e.g., Ma'arrat An Nu'man with 62 fatalities, Saraqab with 61 fatalities). The focus in the data shifts more towards ongoing troop movements, arrests, security measures (e.g., conscription drives by Syrian regime), disrupted weapons use, and civilian displacement. UXO incidents continued to be a threat.

**2021:** This year presents fewer high-fatality events in the sample, with one major Global Coalition airstrike against IS in Iraq's Hamrin Mountains (60 fatalities). Most events relate to military movements by various actors (Coalition, Russia, Syria, QSD, Fatemiyoun Brigade), security measures, arrests by Syrian forces, and continued UXO-related deaths, particularly among Syrian civilians in Lebanon.

**2022:** The pattern of reduced high-fatality events continues in this sample. Incidents are largely characterized by movement of forces (Global Coalition, Russia, HTS, Syria), arrests by Syrian and Turkish police forces, and UXO explosions causing isolated fatalities in Syria and Lebanon. There's a notable withdrawal of Coalition combat personnel from Iraq.

**2023:** A significant resurgence of high-fatality incidents marks this year. IS militants launched devastating attacks targeting truffle collectors in Homs and Hama badia, killing dozens of civilians and QDW members (68 in Wadi Dabiyat, 36 in As-Salamiyeh/Oqeirbat, 24 in Sokhneh, 42 in Al-Rasafah). Israeli warplanes conducted airstrikes on Syrian and pro-Iranian militia positions in Damascus and Deir-ez-Zor, causing multiple fatalities. Turkish warplanes targeted QSD/Asayish facilities, resulting in high casualties (40 in Hamza Bek, 30 in Hisn Castle). A drone strike on Homs Military Academy killed 84. Armed clashes between Syrian forces, HTS, and various rebel factions also led to numerous deaths. The data indicates that despite shifts in conflict intensity, the region remains prone to sudden, lethal eruptions of violence.

## Severity Assessment

*   **Historical Severity (0-100): 100.0**
    This metric reflects an extremely severe and violent history of conflict. The data provides ample evidence of widespread large-scale fatalities, including multiple events exceeding 50, 60, 70, and even over 200 civilian and combatant deaths in single incidents (e.g., Mosul, Deir-ez-Zor, Baghuz). The documented use of chemical weapons, mass executions in prisons, and the continuous involvement of highly lethal non-state actors like IS, alongside protracted multi-actor conventional warfare, fully justifies a maximum historical severity rating. The sheer scale and brutality of events in 2017-2018, in particular, underscore this.

*   **Current Severity (0-100): 100.0**
    Despite periods of relative de-escalation in terms of mass-casualty events in the late 2019-2022 sample, the events of 2023 demonstrate an undeniable and immediate return to extreme violence. Incidents such as the IS attacks on truffle collectors (68, 36 fatalities), the drone strike on Homs Military Academy (84 fatalities), and Turkish/Israeli airstrikes (40, 30, 15 fatalities) illustrate that the capacity for large-scale, lethal events remains fully intact. The ongoing armed clashes between various factions and the targeting of civilians confirm that the underlying conflict dynamics continue to manifest in exceptionally violent ways, meriting a maximum current severity rating.

*   **Escalation Risk (0-100): 92.6**
    The high escalation risk is justified by the persistent and complex interplay of numerous armed actors, both internal and external, across the region. The data shows continuous troop movements, reinforcements, cross-border strikes (Israel, Turkey), and the enduring presence of highly motivated and violent non-state actors like IS and HTS. Unresolved territorial disputes, proxy conflicts, and the propensity for rapid shifts in alliances and frontlines create an environment where local incidents can quickly trigger broader engagements. The frequent discovery and detonation of improvised explosive devices (IEDs) and explosive remnants of war (ERWs) also contribute to a pervasive climate of danger and unpredictability, indicating a very high probability of further escalation and intensification of conflict.

## Strategic Impact & Humanitarian Consequences

The prolonged and severe conflict documented in this sample has had profound strategic and humanitarian repercussions across the Levant and Mesopotamia:

**Strategic Impact:**
The region remains a theater for intense geopolitical competition, drawing in major global and regional powers (US, Russia, Turkey, Iran, Israel). This has led to the fragmentation of state authority, particularly in Syria, and the entrenchment of various non-state armed groups, complicating any prospects for lasting peace. The sustained campaigns against IS, while degrading its conventional capabilities, have not eliminated its insurgency, as evidenced by continued lethal attacks. The conflict has facilitated the projection of influence by external actors, shaping regional power balances and perpetuating proxy conflicts that undermine sovereign governance and regional stability. Cross-border incursions and strikes further destabilize international borders and challenge national sovereignties.

**Humanitarian Consequences:**
The human toll has been catastrophic. The data reflects thousands of fatalities, including a significant number of civilians, from direct combat, airstrikes, chemical attacks, and deliberate targeting (e.g., truffle collectors). Mass displacement of populations, particularly Syrian refugees to neighboring countries like Lebanon and internal displacement within Syria, has created protracted humanitarian crises. The destruction of infrastructure, as seen in urban centers like Mosul and Raqqa, has devastated communities and hindered recovery efforts. The widespread presence of explosive remnants of war (ERWs) and IEDs continues to pose a deadly threat to civilians long after major fighting subsides. The systematic arrests and reported deaths in detention centers highlight severe human rights abuses. Overall, the conflict has resulted in profound societal trauma, disrupted essential services, and created a generation scarred by violence and displacement.
